## 1. Main instruments: how they were made, who played them, which god

### Takeaway
Greek music had two families. The **strings (lyre, kithara, barbitos)** belonged to Apollo and were seen as calm and "civilised". The **wind instrument, the aulos**, belonged to Dionysus and was seen as exciting and emotional. The aulos was a **double-reed pipe, not a flute**, even though translations usually call it a "flute". The hydraulis (water organ) is a later Hellenistic invention (3rd century BC) and the ancestor of the pipe organ.

### Cited Findings
**Lyre (lyra / chelys)**
- A hand-held instrument whose sound box was a **tortoise shell (chelys)**, usually with **seven or more strings**. It was tied to the cult of **Apollo** and was the **standard instrument for teaching aristocratic boys** (Landels 1999; Mathiesen 1999) — [Wikipedia, Music of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_ancient_Greece)
- Myth: **Hermes** made the first lyre from a tortoise shell and gave it to Apollo (*Homeric Hymn to Hermes*) — [Wikipedia, Music of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_ancient_Greece)

**Kithara**
- The professional's version of the lyre, played by paid musicians. It had a **wooden box-shaped body**, was **held upright**, and was **played with a plectrum**. Aristotle calls it an *organon technikon* (an instrument needing professional skill) (*Politics* VIII, 1341a) — [Wikipedia, Music of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_ancient_Greece)

**Barbitos (barbiton)**
- A **long-armed, lower-pitched** lyre, thought to come from eastern Ionia. It was **Sappho's main instrument** and is often shown with **satyrs and drinking parties**. An Attic red-figure amphora of **c. 460 BC** shows a woman playing one — [Wikipedia, Music of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_ancient_Greece)

**Aulos**
- Usually a **pair of double-reed pipes** played together, often held in place with a mouth-band (*phorbeia*). Reconstructions suggest a **reedy sound, closer to an oboe or clarinet than a flute**. It was associated with the cult of **Dionysus**. An aulos already appears at a sacrifice on the **Hagia Triada sarcophagus (Crete, c. 1300 BC)** — [Wikipedia, Music of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_ancient_Greece)
- Myth: **Athena** found or invented the aulos (Telestes, 5th c. BC). In the myth of the satyr **Marsyas**, an aulos player, Marsyas challenges Apollo with his lyre, loses, and is flayed. The story symbolises strings beating winds (Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca*) — [Wikipedia, Music of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_ancient_Greece)
- The aulos accompanied **dithyrambs** (choral songs for Dionysus sung by choirs of men and boys), libations (the *spondeion* tune) and sacrifices — [Wikipedia, Music of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_ancient_Greece)

**Syrinx (Pan pipes)**
- A row of **closed pipes of different lengths**, tuned by cutting them and played by blowing across the top. It is the instrument of the shepherd god Pan. The myth of the nymph Syrinx is told in Ovid's *Metamorphoses* — [Wikipedia, Music of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_ancient_Greece)

**Hydraulis (water organ)**
- Credited to the engineer **Ctesibius of Alexandria, 3rd century BC**. Hand pumps push air into a container standing upside down in water, and the water keeps the air pressure steady for the pipes. It was described by Philo of Byzantium (3rd c. BC) and Hero of Alexandria (1st c. AD) (ref.: McKinnon, "Hydraulis", *Grove Music Online*) — [Wikipedia, Water organ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_organ)
- Vitruvius (*De architectura* X.8) and Heron (*Pneumatica* I.42) describe it — [Wikipedia, Music of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_ancient_Greece)
- Surviving remains: the **Dion organ (Greece), 1st century BC**, found **1992**, with 24 pipes, now in the Archaeological Museum of Dion. The **Aquincum organ (Budapest)**, found **1931**, carries an inscription dating it to **AD 228** — [Wikipedia, Water organ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_organ)

**Percussion and other instruments**
- **Tympanon**: a shallow, round frame drum played with the hand or a stick. **Krotala**: clappers like castanets, used in religious dances. **Salpinx**: a bronze trumpet used for **military signals**, and a contest event at the Olympics (heralds and trumpeters) — [Wikipedia, Music of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_ancient_Greece)

### Inferences
- The Apollo/Dionysus contrast (strings = order and reason, aulos = ecstasy and emotion) is a useful frame for students. It is a cultural association, not a strict rule: the aulos was also played at Apollo's Pythian Games and in the theatre (see section 2).

### Gaps
- Exact materials of aulos pipes (reed, bone, wood, ivory, bronze-sheathed) and of kithara arms were not confirmed from a fetched source in this session.
- The Met Heilbrunn essay "Music in Ancient Greece" could not be fetched (HTTP 429), so museum-object examples (specific vases) could not be added from it.

## 2. Where music was used

### Takeaway
Music was part of almost every area of Greek life: worship, sacrifices, the theatre (the tragic chorus came out of the dithyramb), big contests (the **Pythian Games at Delphi began as a music contest**), drinking parties, weddings and funerals, war, and the core of school education.

### Cited Findings
**Religion and festivals**
- Music went with **sacrifices, initiations and worship**, especially in the cults of Apollo and Dionysus. The Greek words for "making music" and "prophesying" overlapped (Quasten 1983, p. 39) — [Wikipedia, Music of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_ancient_Greece)
- The Delphic Hymns were performed at the **Athenian Pythaïs**, a religious procession from Athens to Delphi — [Wikipedia, Delphic Hymns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delphic_Hymns)

**Theatre**
- Aristotle's *Poetics* traces **tragedy back to the dithyramb**, the choral song for Dionysus (Landels 1999, pp. 13–14) — [Wikipedia, Music of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_ancient_Greece)
- A real tragic chorus survives with musical notation: the **Orestes papyrus (Vienna G 2315)** has lines 338–344 of Euripides' *Orestes* (play first staged **408 BC**). The papyrus copy dates to the **3rd/2nd century BC** — [Austrian National Library, Papyrus Museum](https://onb.ac.at/en/museums/papyrus-museum/programme/permanent-exhibition/the-thematic-areas-of-the-exhibition/orestes-by-euripides); [Duke DCLP 59935](https://papyri-prod.lib.duke.edu/dclp/59935)

**Pythian Games at Delphi (for Apollo)**
- The oldest event was **a hymn to Apollo sung to the kithara**. The Games began as a **musical festival**, and athletics were added later — [Wikipedia, Pythian Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythian_Games) (article flagged for missing citations)
- **Reorganised in 586 BC** (some sources give 582 BC) by the Delphic Amphictyony. **Aulos contests** were added: solo aulos, and singing to the aulos. Singing to the aulos was dropped almost at once because its mournful tunes were thought unlucky. From then on the Games were held **every four years** — [Wikipedia, Pythian Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythian_Games); Pausanias 10.7.4–7, discussed in [Almazova, "Pythian nome", Hyperboreus](https://judaica.ch/index.php/Hyperboreus/article/download/12630/15423/59454)
- **Sakadas of Argos** won the aulos contest in **586 BC** (and, by tradition, in 582 and 578 BC) with the ***Pythikos nomos***, an instrumental piece telling the story of **Apollo's fight with the dragon Python**. It is often called one of the earliest examples of "program music" (music that tells a story) — [Wikipedia, Sakadas of Argos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sakadas_of_Argos); [Almazova, Hyperboreus](https://judaica.ch/index.php/Hyperboreus/article/download/12630/15423/59454)

**Olympic Games**
- Music was "omnipresent" at the Pythian and Olympic Games (Ulrich & Pisk 1963, p. 15). The Olympics had a **herald and trumpeter (salpinx) contest**, and an aulos player accompanied the **pentathlon** — [Wikipedia, Music of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_ancient_Greece); [Wikipedia, Pythian Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythian_Games) (Pythocritus of Sicyon)

**Symposium (drinking party)**
- Guests sang drinking songs called ***skolia***, and libation tunes (*spondeion*) were played at the start and end (Landels 1999, p. 8) — [Wikipedia, Music of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_ancient_Greece)

**Weddings and funerals**
- Music was part of weddings and funerals — [Wikipedia, Music of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_ancient_Greece) (no specific dates or sources given)

**War**
- At the **Battle of Mantinea (418 BC)** the **Spartans advanced slowly to the music of many aulos players**. Thucydides (5.70) says this was **not a religious custom but a practical one**, to keep the ranks in step and stop the line breaking apart — [Livius.org, Thucydides 5.70 translation](https://livius.org/sources/content/thucydides-historian/the-battle-of-mantinea-418-bce)
- The aulos and percussion also kept **rowers on warships in time**, and **trumpet (salpinx) signals** gave orders on the battlefield (Landels 1999, p. 8) — [Wikipedia, Music of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_ancient_Greece)
- Caution: the "Spartans only used music for war" idea is an oversimplification. The aulos was used in many settings — [Bad Ancient, Spartans and music](https://www.badancient.com/claims/spartans-music/)

**Education (paideia)**
- School had two main parts, **music (*mousikē*) and gymnastics** (Pottier 1908, p. 78). Aristotle (*Politics* VIII) argued music should be taught along with reading, writing and gymnastics. In his view, "ethical" melodies belong in education, while very technical instruments (aulos, professional kithara) do not — [Wikipedia, Music of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_ancient_Greece)

### Inferences
- In one line for students: in Greece, music was not just entertainment. It was religion, sport, theatre, school subject and even military technology.

### Gaps
- No primary-source citation was retrieved for music at weddings (*hymenaios*) or funerals (*threnos*, laments). These are standard genres in the scholarly literature (West 1992), but were not verified here.
- Musical contests at the Athenian Panathenaia (kithara and aulos competitions with prize amphoras) were not verified in this session.

## 3. "Mousikē", the Muses and where the word "music" comes from

### Takeaway
Our word "music" comes, through Latin *musica*, from Greek ***mousikē (technē)***, "**the art of the Muses**". For the Greeks, *mousikē* was much wider than "music" is today. It meant **song, poetry, dance and playing instruments together**, and in education almost "culture" in general.

### Cited Findings
- *Mousikē* is short for ***mousikē technē***, "the art of the Muses", from *mousikos*, from ***Mousa* (Muse)**. English *music* comes from Latin ***mūsica***, from the Greek — [Wiktionary/Kaikki entry μουσική](https://kaikki.org/dictionary/Ancient%20Greek/meaning/%CE%BC/%CE%BC%CE%BF/%CE%BC%CE%BF%CF%85%CF%83%CE%B9%CE%BA%CE%AE.html); [Uncovering Sound, Mousike etymology](https://www.uncoveringsound.com/?p=845)
- In classical Greece *mousikē* meant any art led by the Muses, especially music and lyric poetry. It covered **song, dance, poetry, instrumental accompaniment and solos** — [Uncovering Sound](https://www.uncoveringsound.com/?p=845) (secondary blog; agrees with standard references)
- The Muses were the **daughters of Zeus**. In education, "music" stood for **the whole of the non-physical half of schooling** (Pottier 1908) — [Wikipedia, Music of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_ancient_Greece)
- The name *Mousa* is often linked to the Proto-Indo-European root ***men-* ("to think, mind")**, but this is **uncertain** — [Uncovering Sound](https://www.uncoveringsound.com/?p=845)

### Inferences
- Good hook for Latvian students: Latvian *mūzika*, English *music*, German *Musik* and also *muzejs*/*museum* (*mouseion*, "place of the Muses") all go back to the Greek Muses. The museum link is standard etymology, but it was not separately verified in this session.

### Gaps
- etymonline and Britannica could not be fetched (HTTP 403), so the date of the first English use of "music" (Middle English, c. 13th century) is unverified.
- The number and names of the nine Muses (Hesiod, *Theogony*) were not verified here, e.g. Euterpe (aulos, lyric) and Terpsichore (dance).

## 4. Oldest surviving music: the Seikilos epitaph, the Delphic Hymns and other fragments

### Takeaway
The **Seikilos epitaph** is the **oldest surviving *complete* piece of music with notation** in the world, **not the oldest music overall**. It dates to the **1st or 2nd century AD** (scholars disagree). The **Delphic Hymns (128 BC)** are older but only in fragments, and the Hurrian Hymn from Ugarit (c. 1400 BC) is older still.

### Cited Findings
**Seikilos epitaph**
- What it is: a marble **gravestone (stele)** with a short poem and a **song with Greek letter-notation above the words**. It names **Seikilos**, possibly dedicated to **Euterpe** (who may have been his wife, or the Muse Euterpe). Theme: *enjoy life while you have it, life is short, time takes its toll* — [Wikipedia, Seikilos epitaph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seikilos_epitaph)
- **Date: uncertain.** It is usually given as the **1st or 2nd century AD**. **Pöhlmann & West (2001)** prefer the **2nd century AD**, based on letter shapes compared with inscriptions from AD 127/128 and 149/150. **Mathiesen** argues for the **1st century AD** — [Wikipedia, Seikilos epitaph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seikilos_epitaph)
- **Found c. 1883** at **Tralles, near modern Aydın (Turkey)**, during railway building, by the engineer **Edward Purser**. Published by **W. M. Ramsay (1883)**. Some accounts say Ramsay found it himself — [Wikipedia, Seikilos epitaph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seikilos_epitaph)
- **Strange history**: Purser's wife used it as a **flower-pot stand**, and its base was **sawn flat**, destroying one line that survives only in an earlier rubbing. In 1922 it was taken from Smyrna (İzmir) by the Dutch consul and passed through Istanbul and Stockholm to **The Hague**. In **1966** it was bought by the **National Museum of Denmark, Copenhagen**, where it is on display — [Wikipedia, Seikilos epitaph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seikilos_epitaph); location confirmed by [Classic FM](https://www.classicfm.com/discover-music/latest/oldest-song-in-the-world) and [historyofinformation.com](https://www.historyofinformation.com/detail.php?id=5185)
- Mode: described either as the **Phrygian** octave species or the **Ionian (Iastian)** *tonos*. Scholars disagree — [Wikipedia, Seikilos epitaph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seikilos_epitaph)
- Guinness World Records lists it as the "oldest complete song" — [Guinness World Records](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/726551-oldest-complete-song)
- Caveat: older notated music exists, such as the **Hurrian songs from Ugarit (c. 14th century BC)** and the Delphic Hymns, **but only as fragments**. Seikilos is **complete but very short** — [Wikipedia, Seikilos epitaph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seikilos_epitaph); [Aleteia, Hurrian hymn](https://aleteia.org/2018/07/18/this-ancient-sumarian-hymn-is-the-worlds-oldest-complete-musical-composition)

**Delphic Hymns**
- Two hymns to Apollo carved in stone at **Delphi**, found **1893** by the French archaeologist **Théophile Homolle** on the wall of the **Athenian Treasury**. The text was restored by Henri Weil and the music transcribed by **Théodore Reinach** — [Wikipedia, Delphic Hymns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delphic_Hymns)
- Composers: **First Hymn – Athenaios son of Athenaios**. **Second Hymn – Limenios son of Thoinos**, an Athenian. The **Second Hymn is dated precisely to 128 BC**. Older scholarship dated the First c. 138 BC, but recent scholarship places both at the **Athenian Pythaïs of 128 BC** (Pöhlmann & West 2001; Bélis 1992) — [Wikipedia, Delphic Hymns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delphic_Hymns)
- The First Hymn uses **vocal notation** and the Second uses **instrumental notation**. They are among the earliest surviving notated music with **known composers**. Now in the **Delphi Archaeological Museum** — [Wikipedia, Delphic Hymns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delphic_Hymns)

**Other surviving fragments (selection)**
- **Orestes papyrus** (Euripides, *Orestes* 338–344). It is a copy from the **3rd/2nd c. BC**, found in mummy wrappings at Hermopolis (Egypt) and published by **Karl Wessely in 1892**. It is now in the Papyrus Museum, Austrian National Library, Vienna. **It is uncertain whether the melody is Euripides' own** — [ONB Papyrus Museum](https://onb.ac.at/en/museums/papyrus-museum/programme/permanent-exhibition/the-thematic-areas-of-the-exhibition/orestes-by-euripides); [Duke DCLP](https://papyri-prod.lib.duke.edu/dclp/59935)
- Others: Leiden papyrus P. 510 (Euripides, *Iphigenia in Aulis*); the Epidaurus *Hymn to Asclepius*; and the **hymns of Mesomedes** (2nd c. AD, Hadrian's court) — [Wikipedia, Music of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_ancient_Greece)
- In total only a few dozen fragments survive. The standard edition is **Pöhlmann & West, *Documents of Ancient Greek Music* (Oxford 2001)** — cited in [Wikipedia, Delphic Hymns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delphic_Hymns)

### Inferences
- The brief gives "128/127 BC" for the Delphic Hymns, and Wikipedia gives "128 BC". The difference is probably because the Athenian year (archon year) ran from summer to summer and so covers parts of two modern years. For a school talk, the safe wording is "**c. 128 BC**" or "the 2nd century BC". This explanation is an inference and was not confirmed by a source.
- Safe wording for Seikilos: "**about 2,000 years old, from the 1st–2nd century AD**".

### Gaps
- The National Museum of Denmark's own web page and inventory number could not be retrieved. Location is confirmed by several secondary sources and the Wikipedia history, but not by the museum itself.
- The exact count of surviving fragments (often given as "about 60") was not verified.

## 5. Theory: Pythagoras and ratios, modes, ethos (Plato and Aristotle), notation

### Takeaway
The Greeks were the first known culture to **describe musical intervals as number ratios**: octave **2:1**, fifth **3:2**, fourth **4:3**. They built a theory of **modes (*harmoniai*)** and believed music shapes character (**ethos**). They also had **letter-based notation**. Much of what Europe later called "music theory" reached the Middle Ages through **Boethius (c. AD 500)**.

### Cited Findings
**Pythagoras and ratios**
- Tradition credits **Pythagoras (6th c. BC) or the Pythagoreans** with discovering that consonant intervals match simple ratios: **octave 2:1, fifth 3:2, fourth 4:3**. On a string (monochord) at the same tension, halving the length raises the pitch an octave, so the length ratios are the reverse: 1:2, 2:3 and 3:4 — [Wikipedia, Pythagorean hammers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_hammers) (citing Weiss & Taruskin 1984; Christensen, *Cambridge History of Western Music Theory* 2002)
- **The famous blacksmith-hammer story is a legend and physically wrong.** The story says Pythagoras heard hammers weighing 12, 9, 8 and 6 units ring out consonant intervals. The earliest version is from **Nicomachus (c. AD 100)**, about 600 years after Pythagoras, and it was repeated by Iamblichus and **Boethius**. Hammer pitch does not follow weight ratios, and string pitch goes with the **square root** of tension. Historian **Walter Burkert** even dates the monochord to after Pythagoras' time — [Wikipedia, Pythagorean hammers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_hammers)
- The Pythagoreans also held the idea of the **"music of the spheres"** (Landels 1999). **Aristoxenus** (4th c. BC, a pupil of Aristotle) argued that the **ear**, not only mathematics, should judge intervals — [Wikipedia, Music of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_ancient_Greece)

**Modes and ethos (Plato, Aristotle)**
- In **Plato's *Republic* (Book III, 398e)**, Socrates and Glaucon reject the "**mixed Lydian**" and "**tense Lydian**" modes as **dirge-like and useless even for women, let alone men**. They also reject certain "**Ionian and Lydian**" modes as **soft and suited to drinking**, "unbefitting guardians" — [Perseus, Plato Republic 3.398](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Plat.+Rep.+3.398)
- (In the next section, 399a–c, Plato keeps **Dorian** (courage) and **Phrygian** (calm, moderation). That part of the text did not load in this session, but it is the standard reading of the passage.)
- Aristotle (*Politics* VIII) separates "**ethical**" melodies, suited to education, from melodies of "passion" and "action" — [Wikipedia, Music of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_ancient_Greece)

**Names of modes: a common confusion**
- The **medieval church modes reused the Greek names but attached them to different scales**. Medieval theorists apparently thought the Greeks counted scales upward, when they counted downward. So **Greek Dorian ≈ E–E**, but **medieval/modern "Dorian" = D–D**. Greek Phrygian ≈ D–D, but medieval "Phrygian" = E–E — [Britannica, Mode (music): Plainchant](https://www.britannica.com/art/mode-music/Plainchant) (seen via search snippet)
- The error is usually blamed on a **misreading of Boethius** — [Dorian mode (Wikipedia archive)](https://wikipedia.classicistranieri.com/en/articles/d/o/r/Dorian_mode.html)

**Notation**
- The Greeks wrote notes using **letters of the alphabet** (and altered letter shapes) above the text, with **separate vocal and instrumental systems**. Rhythm was marked with extra signs, such as the dots (*stigmai*) on Seikilos, whose meaning is still debated — [Wikipedia, Seikilos epitaph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seikilos_epitaph); [Wikipedia, Delphic Hymns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delphic_Hymns); [ONB Papyrus Museum](https://onb.ac.at/en/museums/papyrus-museum/programme/permanent-exhibition/the-thematic-areas-of-the-exhibition/orestes-by-euripides)

**Transmission to medieval Europe**
- **Boethius, *De institutione musica* (early 6th c. AD)**, was "the most revered authority on music in the Middle Ages". It was compiled from Greek sources, mainly a **lost treatise by Nicomachus** and **Ptolemy's *Harmonics***. Its division of music into *musica mundana / humana / instrumentalis* (cosmic, human, instrumental) was widely quoted — [Norton, *A History of Western Music*, ch. 2 (Burkholder, Grout, Palisca)](https://nerd.wwnorton.com/ebooks/epub/hwm10/EPUB/content/1.2.6-chapter02.xhtml) (seen via search snippet)

### Inferences
- For students, the ratio idea (2:1, 3:2, 4:3) is the clearest "first": it is the start of treating music as something you can **measure and calculate**. This line of thought leads directly to medieval and modern music theory and even physics (acoustics).

### Gaps
- Pythagoras left no writings, and the "discovery" is credited to him only by later tradition. No source was retrieved that says outright that the Babylonians did not know such ratios, so avoid saying "Pythagoras invented harmony".
- The full Plato 399a–c text (Dorian/Phrygian kept, aulos banned, lyre/kithara kept, syrinx for shepherds, "Apollo over Marsyas") did not load and should be checked in any translation before quoting.

## 6. Why Ancient Greece is a fair "starting point" for Western music, and what to be careful with

### Takeaway
Greece is a fair starting point **for the *Western* tradition**, but **not for music itself**. The case rests on concrete things that survive and can be traced: the **word "music"**, the first **music theory based on number ratios**, the **mode names**, **notation**, real **notated pieces** you can perform today (Seikilos), and a **direct written path from Greek theory through Boethius into medieval European music**.

### Cited Findings (arguments FOR the choice)
- **Word and concept**: "music" comes from *mousikē*, "art of the Muses" — [Kaikki/Wiktionary](https://kaikki.org/dictionary/Ancient%20Greek/meaning/%CE%BC/%CE%BC%CE%BF/%CE%BC%CE%BF%CF%85%CF%83%CE%B9%CE%BA%CE%AE.html)
- **Theory**: interval ratios 2:1, 3:2, 4:3 credited to the Pythagoreans — [Wikipedia, Pythagorean hammers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_hammers)
- **Transmission**: Boethius passed Greek music theory (Nicomachus, Ptolemy) to medieval Europe and was its top authority — [Norton, *A History of Western Music*, ch. 2](https://nerd.wwnorton.com/ebooks/epub/hwm10/EPUB/content/1.2.6-chapter02.xhtml)
- **Terms that survive**: the mode names (Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian...) are still taught today, though medieval theorists attached them to different scales — [Britannica, Mode: Plainchant](https://www.britannica.com/art/mode-music/Plainchant)
- **Instruments with descendants**: the hydraulis is the forerunner of the pipe organ — [Wikipedia, Water organ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_organ)
- **Real pieces survive with notation and known composers** (Delphic Hymns 128 BC; Seikilos 1st–2nd c. AD) — [Wikipedia, Delphic Hymns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delphic_Hymns); [Wikipedia, Seikilos epitaph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seikilos_epitaph)
- **Ideas about music's effect on people** (ethos, music in education, Plato and Aristotle) shaped European thinking about music for centuries — [Perseus, Plato Republic 3.398](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Plat.+Rep.+3.398); [Wikipedia, Music of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_ancient_Greece)

### Common claims that are WRONG or OVERSIMPLIFIED (flag for the script)
1. **"The Seikilos epitaph is the oldest song in the world."** It is the oldest **complete** notated piece. Older notated fragments exist, such as the **Hurrian Hymn from Ugarit, c. 1400 BC** — [Wikipedia, Seikilos epitaph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seikilos_epitaph); [Aleteia](https://aleteia.org/2018/07/18/this-ancient-sumarian-hymn-is-the-worlds-oldest-complete-musical-composition). (Aleteia's headline wrongly calls the Hurrian hymn "Sumerian" and "complete". It is Hurrian and fragmentary, which shows how muddled popular sources are.)
2. **Seikilos dates.** Popular sites give anything from "200 BC" to "2nd c. AD". The scholarly range is **1st–2nd century AD**, with Pöhlmann & West favouring the 2nd — [Wikipedia, Seikilos epitaph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seikilos_epitaph)
3. **"Aulos = flute."** Translations of Thucydides and Plato often say "flute", but the aulos was a **reed instrument** (like an oboe), usually played as a double pipe — [Wikipedia, Music of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_ancient_Greece); compare the "flute players" translation at [Livius.org](https://livius.org/sources/content/thucydides-historian/the-battle-of-mantinea-418-bce)
4. **"Pythagoras discovered harmony with blacksmiths' hammers."** This is a late legend (Nicomachus, c. AD 100) and physically impossible as told. The ratios themselves (2:1, 3:2, 4:3) are correct for string lengths and frequencies — [Wikipedia, Pythagorean hammers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_hammers)
5. **"Today's Dorian or Phrygian mode is the Greek one."** It is not. Medieval theorists reused the names for different scales — [Britannica, Mode: Plainchant](https://www.britannica.com/art/mode-music/Plainchant)
6. **"Greek music = Western music began here."** Music is far older (Mesopotamia, Egypt, prehistoric bone flutes), and Greek music itself drew on the Near East (e.g. the barbitos seen as "east-Ionian/foreign", Lydian/Phrygian names from Anatolian peoples). Greece is the start of the **documented Western tradition**, not of music — [Wikipedia, Music of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_ancient_Greece); [Wikipedia, Seikilos epitaph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seikilos_epitaph)
7. **"We know how Greek music sounded."** Only short fragments survive, and even Seikilos' rhythm signs and mode are debated, so modern performances are reconstructions — [Wikipedia, Seikilos epitaph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seikilos_epitaph)
8. **"The Euripides fragment is Euripides' own music."** The papyrus is about 200 years later than the play, and the melody's authorship is uncertain — [ONB Papyrus Museum](https://onb.ac.at/en/museums/papyrus-museum/programme/permanent-exhibition/the-thematic-areas-of-the-exhibition/orestes-by-euripides)
9. **"Spartans only used music for war"** is an oversimplification — [Bad Ancient](https://www.badancient.com/claims/spartans-music/)
10. **"The Delphic Hymns are 2nd century AD."** One tool summary garbled them this way. Correct: **2nd century BC (128 BC)** — [Wikipedia, Delphic Hymns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delphic_Hymns)

### Inferences
- Suggested framing for a 1.5-minute narration: *"Music is older than Greece, but the Greeks gave Europe its word for music, its first theory of music in numbers, and some of the oldest songs we can still play today."* This is defensible against every caveat above.
- Possible narrative arc: Muses/word → instruments (Apollo's lyre vs Dionysus' aulos) → music everywhere (Delphi contests, theatre, Spartans at Mantinea 418 BC, school) → numbers (2:1, 3:2, 4:3) → the Seikilos song, "while you live, shine" (1st–2nd c. AD, now in Copenhagen) → why it matters (Boethius passed it to medieval Europe).

### Gaps
- The Grove/Oxford Music Online article "Greece, ancient" (Mathiesen et al.) and M. L. West's *Ancient Greek Music* (1992) could not be read directly (paywalled). Their claims appear here only through citing pages.
- Polyphony: West (1992, pp. 206–207, as cited by Wikipedia) suggests the Orestes fragment may call for more than one note at once. Otherwise Greek music is generally described as mainly **monophonic** (one melodic line, sometimes with heterophonic accompaniment). This general point was not verified from a fetched scholarly source.
