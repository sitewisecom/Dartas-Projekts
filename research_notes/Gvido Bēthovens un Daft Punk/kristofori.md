# Fact-check: Cristofori and the invention of the piano

Checked 2026-11-05. Main sources: Met essay "The Pianofortes of Bartolomeo Cristofori" (https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-piano-the-pianofortes-of-bartolomeo-cristofori-1655-1731), Britannica, Met/Smithsonian pages. Note: metmuseum.org rate-limited direct fetches (HTTP 429), so the Met wording was checked through search excerpts of the Met pages, not a full read.

1. **TRUE (small nuance).** Born in Padua in 1655, died in Florence in 1731. The Met says he was appointed in 1688 to look after Prince Ferdinando de' Medici's harpsichords. Wording: "keeper (curator) of the instruments and builder for Prince Ferdinando de' Medici from 1688." Britannica gives the death year as 1732 in one place and 1731 in another; use 1731, as the Met does. https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-piano-the-pianofortes-of-bartolomeo-cristofori-1655-1731 ; https://www.britannica.com/biography/Bartolomeo-Cristofori
2. **TRUE.** The 1700 Medici inventory lists "un Arpicembalo di Bartolomeo Cristofori, di nuova inventione, che fa il piano e il forte". The instrument had hammers and dampers. This is the earliest documentary evidence, so "around 1700" is correct. The older date of "1709" found in some books, including Britannica, comes from Maffei's article and is out of date. https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-piano-the-pianofortes-of-bartolomeo-cristofori-1655-1731 ; https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/En_Route_to_the_Piano_A_Converted_Virginal_The_Metropolitan_Museum_Journal_v_13_1978.pdf
3. **NUANCE.** Cristofori's own name for it was "arpicembalo" (the 1700 inventory). "Gravicembalo col piano e forte" ("harpsichord with soft and loud") is the name Scipione Maffei used in 1711. "Pianoforte" and "piano" came from that name. Wording: "In 1711 a writer described it as a 'gravicembalo col piano e forte', which gave us the names pianoforte and piano." https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-piano-the-pianofortes-of-bartolomeo-cristofori-1655-1731
4. **TRUE.** A harpsichord's jacks pluck the strings with plectra, traditionally quill (sometimes leather). Pressing a key harder barely changes the loudness. https://www.britannica.com/art/harpsichord ; https://www.metmuseum.org/perspectives/inventing-cristofori-piano
5. **TRUE (add detail).** Small hammers covered with leather strike the strings. The escapement lets each hammer fall away at once so the string keeps sounding. A "check" catches the hammer so it does not bounce back. Dampers silence the string when the key is let go. https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-piano-the-pianofortes-of-bartolomeo-cristofori-1655-1731 ; https://www.britannica.com/art/piano
6. **NUANCE.** Maffei's article, "Nuova invenzione d'un gravecembalo col piano e forte", appeared in Giornale de' letterati d'Italia vol. 5 (1711) with a woodcut diagram of the action. It reached Silbermann through Johann Mattheson's German translation in "Critica musica" (1725). Silbermann probably read it there and began building pianos in the 1730s. Wording: "...which, translated into German in 1725, inspired Gottfried Silbermann." https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-piano-the-pianofortes-of-bartolomeo-cristofori-1655-1731
7. **TRUE.** The three survivors are 1720 (Metropolitan Museum, New York, the oldest surviving piano), 1722 (Museo Nazionale degli Strumenti Musicali, Rome) and 1726 (Musikinstrumenten-Museum, Leipzig University). https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/501788 ; https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-piano-the-pianofortes-of-bartolomeo-cristofori-1655-1731
8. **TRUE (general).** Touch now controlled loudness, hence "piano e forte". The piano later became the central instrument for composing, teaching and music at home. Mozart and Beethoven wrote for and played the fortepiano, the Viennese pianos of Stein, Walter and Graf. Avoid implying that they played Cristofori's own instruments. https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-piano-viennese-instruments ; https://www.britannica.com/art/piano
