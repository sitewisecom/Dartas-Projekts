# Guido of Arezzo (Gvido no Areco): life, staff, solmization, significance

Research notes for a ~70 s narrated Latvian school animation (4 beats). All dates in the Middle Ages are approximate unless stated. Key primary source used: Guido's own *Epistola ad Michaelem* ("Letter to Brother Michael on an unknown chant") in Michael Hermesdorff's 1884 German translation (copy at kilchb.de, originally from henk-reints.nl).

## 1. Who Guido was and the time he lived in

### Takeaway
Guido was an Italian Benedictine monk and music teacher, born c. 991–992 (scholars' estimates range c. 990–999) and died some time after May 1033. He trained at Pomposa Abbey, taught the singers at the cathedral of Arezzo, and was called to Rome by Pope John XIX c. 1028. He lived at a time when church singers had to learn Gregorian chant by ear, from a teacher, over about 10 years.

### Cited Findings
- **Birth:** c. 991–992 (Palisca, *Grove* 2001, based on Oesch dating the *Micrologus* to 1025–26). Other estimates: 992–994 (Atkinson 2008) and 994–998 (Smits van Waesberghe). The broad range given is 990–999. — [Wikipedia: Guido of Arezzo, citing Grove/Palisca, Atkinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guido_of_Arezzo)
- **Death:** after 20 May 1033. That is the date of the last document that places him in Arezzo. No exact death date is known. — [Wikipedia, citing Ruini 2004; Palisca 2001](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guido_of_Arezzo)
- **Birthplace is disputed:** Arezzo or the area around Pomposa. Sigebert of Gembloux calls him "Guido Aretinus". — [Wikipedia, citing Mafucci 2003](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guido_of_Arezzo)
- **Pomposa:** he entered Pomposa Abbey (near Ferrara, on the Adriatic coast) c. 1013. He left c. 1025 because fellow monks opposed him, including Abbot Guido of Pomposa. — [Wikipedia, citing Britannica 2021; Ruini 2004](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guido_of_Arezzo)
- **Arezzo:** from c. 1025 he worked under Bishop Tedald (Theodaldus, bishop 1023–1036) and trained the singers of the cathedral. He dedicated the *Micrologus* to Tedald. — [Wikipedia, citing Palisca 2001](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guido_of_Arezzo)
- **Rome, c. 1028:** Pope John XIX (pope 1024–1033) called him to Rome. In his own letter Guido says the pope "heard of our school and how, with the help of our antiphoners, boys were learning chants they had never heard". The pope sent three messengers to summon him. Guido travelled with Abbot Grunwald and Peter, provost of Arezzo cathedral. The pope looked through the antiphoner "like a marvel" and did not get up from his seat until he had himself learned a verse he had never heard sung. Guido had to leave Rome quickly because of illness and the summer heat in the marshy city. — [Guido, Epistola ad Michaelem (Hermesdorff transl. 1884)](https://www.kilchb.de/guido.pdf); [Wikipedia, citing Palisca; Ruini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guido_of_Arezzo)
- After Rome, Abbot Guido of Pomposa saw the antiphoner, regretted siding with Guido's "envious rivals", and invited him back to Pomposa. — [Epistola (Hermesdorff)](https://www.kilchb.de/guido.pdf)
- **Later life:** from c. 1029 he probably lived in a Camaldolese monastery (Avellana) near Arezzo. — [Wikipedia, citing Britannica; Palisca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guido_of_Arezzo)
- **The era:** chant teaching was by ear. Guido writes that singers should not always depend "on the voice of a precentor or the sound of an instrument, like the blind who cannot walk without a guide". — [Epistola (Hermesdorff)](https://www.kilchb.de/guido.pdf)
- **Neumes:** the early neumes "did not specify exact pitches but only the shape of the melodies". They probably served as memory aids for melodies learned by rote. — [Wikipedia: Staff (music)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staff_(music))
- **10 years:** Guido himself says that until then teachers "could barely acquire, in ten years, an imperfect knowledge of chant". — [Epistola (Hermesdorff)](https://www.kilchb.de/guido.pdf)
- **Works and dates (all approximate):**
  - *Micrologus*: c. 1025–1026, dedicated to Bishop Tedald.
  - *Regulae rhythmicae*: after 1026.
  - *Prologus in antiphonarium*: begun at Pomposa, finished c. 1030.
  - *Epistola ad Michaelem* (= *Epistola de ignoto cantu*): c. 1028 (Palisca) to c. 1032 (Atkinson), no later than 1033.
  — [Wikipedia, citing Palisca, Atkinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guido_of_Arezzo)
- **Name confusion:** a separate 11th-century letter on simony (*Epistola Widonis*, c. 1031) is also attributed to a Guido of Arezzo. Don't confuse it with the music letter. — [White Rose eprints](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/169771/3/The%20simony%20crisis%20of%20the%20eleventh%20century%20and%20the%20Epistola%20Widonis.pdf)

### Inferences
- Safe narration wording: "Around the year 1000 / in the early 11th century, the Italian monk Guido…" and "born around 991, died after 1033".
- The Pope John XIX scene, where the pope learns an unknown chant in one sitting, comes from Guido's own letter, so it is a good, verifiable story moment. Note that it is Guido's own account and may be self-promotional.
- "Benedictine": Pomposa was a Benedictine abbey and Guido calls himself a monk. Standard references describe him as a Benedictine monk.

### Gaps
- Britannica returned 403 and could not be read directly. Its content is cited only through Wikipedia's references to "Britannica 2021".
- I could not find an exact birth date or place. Both are uncertain in all scholarship.

## 2. How he improved notation: staff lines that fix pitch

### Takeaway
Guido did not invent the very first musical line. Earlier scribes used single dry-point or ink lines, letter notations (Hucbald, the *Musica enchiriadis*'s daseian signs) and heighted neumes. His achievement was to combine these into a consistent system. He placed neumes on lines and in spaces a third apart, marked the F line in red and the C line in yellow, and/or wrote a letter (clef) at the start of a line. This made every note's pitch exact, so a singer could read a melody they had never heard. He describes this in the *Prologus in antiphonarium*.

### Cited Findings
- **Before Guido:**
  - **Heighted neumes:** from the second quarter of the 10th century, "diastematic" (heighted) neumes began to show pitch and intervals.
  - **Single lines:** some 10th–11th-century French (Aquitanian) manuscripts used a single line, sometimes "not more than a dry-pointed line scratched into the parchment", sometimes in ink, marking a central note such as the final.
  — [Univ. of Basel, "Lines and staff" (TALES project)](https://tales.nmc.unibas.ch/en/from-ink-to-sound-32/from-sound-to-ink-early-forms-of-musical-notation-188/lines-and-staff-949)
- **Daseian notation:** *Musica enchiriadis* (c. 900) used daseian notation to show specific pitches. Some manuscripts used "one or more horizontal lines to indicate particular pitches". — [Wikipedia: Staff (music)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staff_(music))
- **Hucbald** (c. 840–930) "used lines and the first letters of the Latin alphabet as a means of fixing the intervals of the scale", which makes him a forerunner of Guido. His authorship of *Musica enchiriadis* / daseian notation is disputed and is now often given to an anonymous "Pseudo-Hucbald". — [New Advent / Catholic Encyclopedia: Hucbald](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/07510b.htm); [Britannica Kids: Hucbald](https://kids.britannica.com/students/article/Hucbald-or-Hubaldus/326780)
- **Coloured lines:** Italian manuscripts of the 11th century used two coloured lines, "a red one to indicate the F and a yellow one to indicate the C as both notes are above a semi-tone". Latin letters were placed as clefs "to determine the pitch". "Guido of Arezzo (c. 991–1033) claimed that he had invented this system." — [Univ. of Basel TALES](https://tales.nmc.unibas.ch/en/from-ink-to-sound-32/from-sound-to-ink-early-forms-of-musical-notation-188/lines-and-staff-949)
- **More lines:** next, "a third line was introduced between the lines of F and C" and "a fourth drawn above these three lines", which "permitted to display a fully diastematic notation". A Roman manuscript of 1071 (Cod. Bodmer 74) still shows red F lines; its yellow C lines have faded. — [Univ. of Basel TALES](https://tales.nmc.unibas.ch/en/from-ink-to-sound-32/from-sound-to-ink-early-forms-of-musical-notation-188/lines-and-staff-949)
- **Thirds and clefs:** the core innovation was a staff built on intervals of a third, with clefs given by Latin letters or by yellow (C) and red (F) lines. Guido also said that lines with the same letter or colour sound the same throughout the antiphoner. The number of lines was not fixed; four lines with movable F and C clefs became the norm later. — [search summary of Scribd paper "The contribution of Guido of Arezzo to Western music theory"](https://es.scribd.com/document/167435247/The-contribution-of-Guido-of-Arezzo-to-Western-music-theory) (secondary; wording should be checked against Pesce's 1999 critical edition of the *Prologus*)
- **The four-line staff today:** Guido's four-line staff "is still used (though without the red and yellow coloring he recommended)" in Gregorian chant books today. Five-line staves appeared in Italy in the 13th century, but staves of 4, 5 and 6 lines were used until about 1600. — [Wikipedia: Staff (music), citing Harvard Dictionary of Music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staff_(music))
- **The *Prologus*:** the Russian Great Encyclopedia describes the *Prologus in antiphonarium* as the text where linear (staff) notation is first described. — [Bigenc.ru](https://bigenc.ru/b/gvido-aretinskii-prolog-k-ba94be)

### Inferences
- Wording to avoid overclaiming:
  - Avoid: "Guido invented the staff."
  - Use: "Guido turned scattered experiments into a clear system of lines and spaces, so each note had an exact pitch."
- Visual idea for the beat: a plain neume "wave" with no lines, then a red F line, then a yellow C line, then a letter clef, then 4 lines.
- The colours are exactly what Latvian children would see as red (sarkana) for F and yellow (dzeltena) for C.

### Gaps
- I could not access an English translation of the *Prologus* (Pesce 1999, or Strunk's *Source Readings*) to quote Guido's exact words on lines and colours. The colour assignments (red = F, yellow/saffron = C) are consistent across all sources found.
- I found no reliable source on whether the colour convention existed in any form before Guido. One search snippet hinted that some earlier scribes used it inconsistently, but this was not verified.

## 3. Solmization: ut–re–mi–fa–sol–la, and how it became do-re-mi

### Takeaway
To help pupils memorise each pitch, Guido used the hymn to St John the Baptist *Ut queant laxis*. Its text is attributed to Paul the Deacon (8th c.), and in the melody Guido used, each of the six half-lines starts one step higher than the last. The opening syllables gave the six note names ut, re, mi, fa, sol, la: a hexachord, with the semitone between mi and fa. Later changes:
- **si** was added for the seventh note (16th–17th c. proposals; dates conflict).
- **ut** became **do** in 17th-century Italy, usually credited to G. B. Doni (first printed mention: Bononcini 1673).
- In English-speaking countries **si** became **ti** in the 19th century (Sarah Glover).

### Cited Findings
- **Guido's method in his own words:** to learn a note, the pupil should notice it at the start of a melody they know perfectly. Guido uses mainly "this melody, which I use for teaching boys", *Ut queant laxis*. It "begins in its six half-lines with six different notes". Anyone who knows the start of each line can then identify those six notes anywhere. — [Epistola (Hermesdorff)](https://www.kilchb.de/guido.pdf)
- **Hymn text:** the text Guido printed in the *Epistola* (syllable used in caps):

  > **UT** queant laxis **RE**sonare fibris
  > **MI**ra gestorum **FA**muli tuorum,
  > **SOL**ve polluti **LA**bii reatum,
  > Sancte Iohannes.

  — [Epistola (Hermesdorff)](https://www.kilchb.de/guido.pdf)
- **English translation:** "So that your servants may, with loosened [voices], resound the wonders of your deeds, clean the guilt from our stained lips, O Saint John." — [Wikipedia: Ut queant laxis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ut_queant_laxis)
- **Context:** it is a Sapphic-stanza hymn for the feast of the Nativity of St John the Baptist (24 June). Paul the Deacon is generally accepted as the author, though Duemmler doubted it. — [Catholic Encyclopedia (New Advent)](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/15244a.htm)
- **Melody:** each syllable falls on a successive note of the scale (C, D, E, F, G, A). The melody may be older than Guido: the Catholic Encyclopedia says it appears in a 10th-century manuscript set to Horace's ode to Phyllis. Wikipedia says a variant appears in an 11th-century French setting of Horace's Ode 4.11, and that Guido "possibly composed" the melody but "more likely used an existing melody". — [New Advent](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/15244a.htm); [Wikipedia: Ut queant laxis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ut_queant_laxis)
- **Scholarly caveat:** Dom Pothier argued that Guido did not himself give the syllable names to the notes or invent the hexachord system. He held that the names arose gradually. — [New Advent](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/15244a.htm)
- **Hexachord:** Guido "first described the hexachord as a mnemonic device" in the *Epistola* and *Micrologus*. Its six pitches are ut, re, mi, fa, sol, la, with a semitone between mi and fa. — [Wikipedia: Guidonian hand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guidonian_hand)
- **Seven notes:** Guido notes there are only seven different notes, "as there are seven days in the week". — [Epistola (Hermesdorff)](https://www.kilchb.de/guido.pdf)
- **"si":** formed from the initials of "Sancte Iohannes", the last line of the stanza. In 1599 Erich van der Putten (Puteanus) proposed "bi"; most theorists preferred "si". — [New Advent](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/15244a.htm)
- Wikipedia instead says si "was added in the 18th century". **Conflict:** the 1599 date suggests si was already being proposed around 1600. Safest wording: "later, a seventh syllable, si, was added". — [Wikipedia: Ut queant laxis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ut_queant_laxis)
- **"ut" to "do":** "'Ut' was changed in the 1600s in Italy to the open syllable 'Do'". — [Wikipedia: Solfège, citing McNaught 1893](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solf%C3%A8ge)
- **Doni:** Grove's *Dictionary of Music and Musicians* says Fétis credits **G. B. Doni** with "do", and that it is mentioned in Bononcini's *Musico pratico* (1673) as used "per essere più resonante" ("because it is more resonant"). — [Grove (1900) "Do", via Wikisource mirror](https://wiki.1209k.com/content/wikisource_en_all_maxi_2022-09/A/A_Dictionary_of_Music_and_Musicians/Do)
- **Doni's death date conflicts:** the Grove entry says he died in 1669, but the National Library of Israel authority record gives 1593/94–1647. Use 1647. — [NLI](https://nli.org.il/en/authorities/987007281058405171)
- A popular theory says "do" also comes from "Dominus" (Lord) or from Doni's own name. This is speculation. — [search summary; e.g. generalist.academy](https://generalist.academy/2021/07/27/the-do-in-do-re-mi/)
- **"ti":** Sarah Glover changed "si" to "ti" in the 19th century in English-speaking countries, so that each syllable starts with a different letter. — [Wikipedia: Solfège](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solf%C3%A8ge)

### Inferences
- For Latvia: Latvian schools use relative solmization (do, re, mi, fa, sol, la, si, do, plus hand signs in the tradition of the Latvian "relatīvā solmizācija" method). So the "si" ending will feel familiar, and "ti" is the English variant.
- Narration-safe line: "Guido took the first syllables of each line of a hymn to St John (ut, re, mi, fa, sol, la). Centuries later 'ut' became 'do' and 'si' was added, which gives us do-re-mi."

### Gaps
- I found no reliable exact year for the do/ut change or for when "si" became standard. Only "17th century" (do) is solid.
- I was unable to verify whether the Latvian relative-solmization method uses "si" or "ti". The inference above is from general knowledge, so check it locally.

## 4. Why it was significant, plus myths to avoid

### Takeaway
Guido's system of lines, clefs and syllables let singers sight-read, that is, sing correctly a melody they had never heard. By his own claim this cut chorister training from about 10 years to 1–2 years. Some boys could sing unknown melodies by the third day. His staff is the direct ancestor of modern staff notation. The *Micrologus* became one of the most widely copied and commented music textbooks of the Middle Ages.

### Cited Findings
- **Time saved:** Guido writes that earlier teachers "could hardly acquire in ten years an imperfect knowledge of chant", whereas "we form a perfect singer within one, at most two years". — [Epistola (Hermesdorff)](https://www.kilchb.de/guido.pdf); [Wikipedia, citing Ruini 2004](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guido_of_Arezzo)
- **Sight-singing:** "Since I began teaching boys by this method, some could already sing completely unknown melodies with ease on the third day, which otherwise would not have been possible after many weeks." — [Epistola (Hermesdorff)](https://www.kilchb.de/guido.pdf)
- **Legacy (credited by Britannica):** Guido is credited with staff notation, which had a major influence on Western notation. Wikipedia calls him "the father of modern musical notation". — [Wikipedia: Guido of Arezzo, citing Britannica 2021](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guido_of_Arezzo); [Wikipedia: Ut queant laxis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ut_queant_laxis)
- **Influence after his death:** commentaries on the *Micrologus* followed soon after: Aribo (fl. c. 1068–78) and Johannes Cotto (fl. c. 1100). — [Wikipedia, citing Palisca 2001b; Hughes 2001](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guido_of_Arezzo)
- **Monuments:** statues by Lorenzo Nencini (1847, Uffizi loggia, Florence) and Salvino Salvini (1882, Arezzo). — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guido_of_Arezzo)
- **Myth: the Guidonian hand.** He is "somewhat erroneously credited" with the hand. He described only a rudimentary form, and the full hexachord system cannot be securely attributed to him (Palisca, Grove). The hand "does not have the depicted form until the 12th century". The attribution comes from Sigebert of Gembloux (c. 1105–1110), writing decades after Guido. — [Wikipedia: Guido, citing Palisca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guido_of_Arezzo); [Wikipedia: Guidonian hand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guidonian_hand)
- **Myth: "Guido invented the staff / the first lines."** Single lines, letter notations and heighted neumes all existed before him. Guido claimed the coloured-line-and-letter system as his own. — [Univ. of Basel TALES](https://tales.nmc.unibas.ch/en/from-ink-to-sound-32/from-sound-to-ink-early-forms-of-musical-notation-188/lines-and-staff-949)
- **Myth: "the five-line staff."** Guido's staff was not five lines; it was typically four, with no fixed number. Five-line staves came in the 13th century, and line counts were still mixed until about 1600. — [Wikipedia: Staff (music)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staff_(music))
- **Myth: "Guido invented do-re-mi."** His syllables were ut–re–mi–fa–sol–la. "do" and "si" came centuries later, and Pothier even doubted that Guido assigned the syllables himself. — [New Advent](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/15244a.htm); [Grove "Do"](https://wiki.1209k.com/content/wikisource_en_all_maxi_2022-09/A/A_Dictionary_of_Music_and_Musicians/Do)
- **Myth: "Guido wrote the hymn" or "Guido composed the melody."** The text is attributed to Paul the Deacon (8th c.), and the melody probably predates Guido. — [Wikipedia: Ut queant laxis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ut_queant_laxis)
- **Myth: "an 8th-century monk invented do re mi."** Popular sites (e.g. a ChurchPop headline) blur Paul the Deacon (8th c.) with Guido (11th c.). Avoid. — [ChurchPop (example of confusion)](https://www.churchpop.com/the-8th-century-benedictine-monk-who-accidentally-invented-do-re-mi/)
- **Myth: "Guido's dates are 990–1050."** Some pages (e.g. the Wikipedia Staff article) give these dates. They are imprecise; the documented evidence is only "alive in 1033". — [Wikipedia: Staff (music)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staff_(music)); [Wikipedia: Guido](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guido_of_Arezzo)

### Inferences
- Core message for beat 4: before Guido, a song lived only in someone's memory and voice. After Guido, it could be written down so that a stranger, in another place or century, could sing it accurately. This is the root of all later written music, which links naturally to Beethoven and Daft Punk in the wider project.
- The "10 years down to 1–2 years" and "third day" figures are Guido's own claims, so attribute them: "Guido boasted that…" / "according to Guido…".

### Gaps
- I found no independent (non-Guido) contemporary evidence measuring the time savings. The figures come only from his own letter.
- I could not verify how quickly Guido's staff spread across Europe (e.g. dates of adoption north of the Alps) within the tool budget.
